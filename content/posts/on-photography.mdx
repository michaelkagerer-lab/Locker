Photography taught me something I didn't expect: the practice of making images is really a practice of learning to look.

Before I shot seriously, I walked through the world half-present — aware of the broad strokes, missing the texture. Once I started carrying a camera with intent, I noticed I was also seeing more. The way afternoon light cuts across a concrete wall. The geometry of a fire escape against a pale sky. The particular sadness of an empty chair.

## The Camera as a Reason to Stop

There's a social permission that comes with having a camera. It gives you a reason to stand somewhere and stare. Without it, standing and staring at a building for three minutes feels strange. With it, you're just working.

But more than the permission, the camera is a commitment device. When I decide I'm going out to shoot, I'm deciding to pay attention. I'm slow. I double back. I wait for the light to change. The act of photography forces a kind of patience that I find genuinely difficult in other areas of my life.

## What I Actually Shoot

My subjects are unglamorous by most definitions:

- Fog in the morning before anything is open
- The quality of light in a particular street at a particular hour
- Edges — where the built environment meets the natural one
- Shadows that do more work than the objects casting them

I'm less interested in drama than in stillness. The best photographs I've made don't announce themselves; they just sit there, quietly being true to something.

## On Gear

The gear conversation in photography is tedious, so I'll be brief: it matters less than people say. A phone camera used with real intention will produce more interesting work than a full-frame sensor pointed at the obvious thing. The camera doesn't see. You do.

That said, shooting with a camera that has physical controls — aperture ring, focus ring, a shutter that has weight to it — changes your relationship with the process. It slows you down in a useful way.

## The Edit

I spend as much time editing as shooting, maybe more. The edit is where the real decisions get made: what is this image actually about, and is everything else getting in the way?

Most of my photos are discarded in the edit. Not because they're technically bad, but because they're not saying anything. I'd rather have a small number of honest images than a large portfolio of competent ones.
